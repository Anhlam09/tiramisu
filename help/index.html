Help & answers

Answers in plain English

Short answers to the questions families ask most. Tiramisu gives general information, not legal advice.

29 answers and 24 terms

Common questions

What is probate?

Probate is the court process that confirms who is in charge of a person's estate and makes sure debts are paid and belongings go to the right people. It is usually overseen by a local court. Rules differ by state, so check with your court or an attorney.

Related: See your roadmap

Does every estate go through probate?

No. Accounts with a named beneficiary, jointly owned property, and assets held in a trust often pass without probate. Small estates may use a simpler process. Rules differ by state, so check with your court or an attorney.

Related: About this estate, Your inventory

What does an executor do?

An executor, also called a personal representative, gathers the person's belongings, pays valid bills and taxes, and gives what remains to the people named in the will. You act carefully and keep records, but you are not expected to know everything on day one. Many people ask for help along the way.

Related: Your roadmap, Money in and out

What are letters testamentary?

Letters testamentary are a court document that proves you are the executor named in the will. Banks and other institutions often ask for them before they will talk to you about an account. When there is no will, the court issues a similar document called letters of administration. Rules differ by state, so check with your court or an attorney.

Related: Paperwork helper, Documents

What happens if there is no will?

When someone dies without a will, state law decides who inherits and who can be appointed to manage the estate. Usually a spouse and children come first, then other relatives. The court appoints an administrator, often a close family member. Rules differ by state, so check with your court or an attorney.

Related: About this estate

What is a small-estate affidavit?

Many states let families collect a modest amount of property by signing a sworn statement, called an affidavit, instead of opening a full probate case. The dollar limit and waiting period differ a lot from state to state. Rules differ by state, so check with your court or an attorney.

Related: About this estate, Paperwork helper

What are POD and TOD accounts?

Payable-on-death (POD) bank accounts and transfer-on-death (TOD) investment accounts name a person who receives the money directly. The beneficiary usually shows a death certificate and ID to the institution. These accounts typically do not go through probate.

Related: Your inventory

Does life insurance go through probate?

Usually not. Life insurance is paid to the beneficiary named on the policy, who files a claim with the insurer. If no beneficiary is named, or the named person has also died, the money may become part of the estate. Check the policy with the insurer.

Related: Find accounts, Your inventory

Am I responsible for their debts?

In general, debts are paid from the estate, not from your own money. Family members are usually not personally responsible unless they co-signed or held the account jointly. Do not pay a debt from your own funds before talking to someone you trust. Rules differ by state, so check with your court or an attorney.

Related: Your inventory

What is an EIN and why does the estate need one?

An EIN, or Employer Identification Number, is a tax ID the IRS gives to the estate. The estate uses it to open a bank account and file its own tax return, so the person's Social Security number is not used for estate business. It is free and can be requested on the IRS website.

Related: Paperwork helper

Do I need an estate bank account?

Most executors open one. It keeps estate money separate from your own, which makes record keeping much easier. You will usually need the EIN and letters from the court to open it.

Related: Money in and out

How many death certificates should I order?

Many families order 10 to 12 certified copies, since banks, insurers and agencies often want an original. It is cheaper to order them together at the start than to reorder later. Some places accept a photocopy, so ask before you send an original.

Related: First days checklist, Documents

How do I notify Social Security?

A funeral home often reports the death to Social Security for you, so ask them. If not, call the Social Security Administration. Any payment received for the month of death usually has to be returned, and the agency can explain what applies to survivors.

Related: Paperwork helper, First days checklist

Should I contact the credit bureaus?

Yes, it is a good protective step. Ask Equifax, Experian and TransUnion to note the file as deceased, and to send you a copy of the credit report. This helps prevent identity theft, which can happen to people who have died.

Related: First days checklist

How can I protect against identity theft?

Keep several copies of the death certificate in a safe place, share the Social Security number only when needed, and watch the credit report for new accounts. Cancel cards and services that are no longer needed. If you see something unfamiliar, contact the company and the credit bureaus.

Related: First days checklist, Paperwork helper

Are there final tax returns to file?

Usually. A final personal return (Form 1040) covers the year of death, and if the estate earns more than a small amount of income afterward, it may also file an estate return (Form 1041). A tax professional can help with both. Rules differ by state, so check with your court or an attorney.

Related: Your roadmap, Paperwork helper

What is the difference between estate tax and inheritance tax?

An estate tax is paid by the estate, and only large estates owe the federal version. An inheritance tax is paid by the person who inherits, and only a few states have one. Most families owe neither. Ask a tax professional about your state.

Related: About this estate

How long does settling an estate take?

Simple estates often take six months to a year. Larger estates, property sales, or disagreements can take longer. Many courts include a waiting period for creditors, so some of the time is just waiting.

Related: Your roadmap

Do I need an attorney?

Not always. Some families handle a small, simple estate on their own, and many court clerks offer forms. An attorney helps most with disputes, real estate, no will, large estates, or business interests. Rules differ by state, so check with your court or an attorney.

How do executors get paid or reimbursed?

You can usually be repaid from the estate for reasonable costs you paid, such as funeral bills, if you keep receipts. Many states also allow a fee for the executor's time, which you may choose to decline. Track every expense as you go. Rules differ by state, so check with your court or an attorney.

Related: Money in and out

What about email and other digital accounts?

Make a list of accounts, such as email, photos, subscriptions and online banking. Many companies have a process for families, and some states have laws about access. Do not guess passwords or sign in as the person. Note where access is kept, never the password itself.

Related: Your inventory, Find accounts

Can I sell the house?

You usually need legal authority first, such as letters from the court, unless the home passes to someone by title. Keep it insured and maintained while you wait. A real estate attorney can explain the steps. Rules differ by state, so check with your court or an attorney.

Related: Your inventory, Your roadmap

What is the creditor notice period?

Many states set a window, often a few months after you are appointed, during which creditors can make a claim against the estate. The estate may need to publish a notice or send letters. The length of the window varies a lot. Rules differ by state, so check with your court or an attorney.

Related: Your roadmap

When can I give things to beneficiaries?

Most executors wait until debts, taxes and the creditor period are settled before giving out the rest. Some gifts, such as a keepsake, can sometimes be given sooner with the court's or an attorney's okay. Keep a record of who received what.

Related: People, Your inventory

How do I close the estate?

When everything has been paid and distributed, you usually file a final accounting or a closing statement with the court, and the court ends your role. Keep your records for several years afterward. Rules differ by state, so check with your court or an attorney.

Related: Money in and out, Export your records

What should I do in the first week?

Take it one step at a time. Ask for several death certificates, secure the home and valuables, collect mail, and find the will. You do not need to do everything at once, and most things can wait a few weeks.

Related: First days checklist, Your roadmap

How do I find the will?

Look in a home safe, a desk, or a safe deposit box, and ask the family attorney or the person's bank. Some courts accept wills for safekeeping. Do not throw away any version of a will, even an old one.

Related: Documents

How can I find accounts we did not know about?

Look through mail, tax returns and bank statements for clues. Then search the free sources for unclaimed property, life insurance policies, savings bonds and retirement plans. Treat any paid service that promises to find money with care.

Related: Find accounts

How can I make this easier for my own family?

Write down where important papers and accounts are, check who is named as beneficiary, and tell someone you trust where to look. A short, clear list can save your family weeks of searching.

Glossary

Administrator
The person a court appoints to manage an estate when there is no will, or when the named executor cannot serve.
Affidavit
A written statement signed under oath. Small-estate affidavits let some families collect property without a full probate case.
Appraisal
A professional opinion of what something is worth, often used for a house, jewelry or art.
Beneficiary
A person or group who receives money or property from a will, account, policy or trust.
Creditor claim
A request from a person or company that is owed money by the estate. The executor reviews each one.
Decedent
The person who has died.
EIN
Employer Identification Number. A tax ID from the IRS that an estate uses for its bank account and tax return.
Estate
Everything a person owned and owed at the time of death.
Executor
The person named in a will to carry out its wishes and settle the estate. Also called a personal representative.
Fiduciary
Someone who must act carefully and honestly for the benefit of others, such as an executor or trustee.
Heir
A person who is entitled by state law to inherit when there is no will.
Intestate
Dying without a valid will. State law then decides who inherits.
K-1
A tax form that shows a beneficiary's share of income from an estate or trust. Beneficiaries use it on their own tax return.
Letters of administration
A court document that gives an administrator authority to act when there is no will.
Letters testamentary
A court document that proves an executor has authority to act for the estate.
Non-probate asset
Property that passes outside the court process, such as accounts with a named beneficiary or jointly owned property.
POD
Payable on death. A bank account that goes to a named person when the owner dies.
Probate
The court process that confirms an executor and oversees paying debts and passing on property.
Probate asset
Property that must go through the court process because no beneficiary or co-owner is named.
Residuary estate
What is left after debts, taxes and specific gifts have been taken care of.
TOD
Transfer on death. An investment account or property that goes to a named person when the owner dies.
Trust
A legal arrangement in which one person, the trustee, holds property for the benefit of others.
Trustee
The person or company who manages the property held in a trust.
Will
A written document that says who should receive a person's property and who should manage the estate.

Talk to a professional

You do not have to do this alone. Some situations are easier with an estate attorney.

When an attorney helps most

  • Someone disputes the will or how the estate is handled
  • There is a house or other real estate to transfer or sell
  • There is no will
  • The estate is large or complicated
  • There is a business interest

Questions to ask an estate attorney

  1. How do you charge: hourly, a flat fee, or a share of the estate?
  2. What costs besides your fee should I expect, such as court fees?
  3. Does this estate need to go through probate at all?
  4. Is there a simpler process for an estate like this one?
  5. About how long do you expect this to take?
  6. What can I do myself to save time and cost?
  7. Who in your office will handle my questions, and how fast do you usually reply?
  8. What documents should I gather before we begin?
  9. Are there deadlines in this state that I should know about?
  10. What could make this more complicated or expensive?

Finding one

The American Bar Association has a Find Legal Help directory (opens in a new tab) that points to lawyer referral services. Many state bar associations also run their own referral services, and some offer free or reduced-fee help.
